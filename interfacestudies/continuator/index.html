Continuator

Continuator is the learning and response algorithm, not the interface. Play through MusEDLab’s simplified Scale Rows interface below; the official Continuator engine learns your phrases and answers.

Try it as a listening experiment

  1. Play two or three different short gestures, leaving a pause after each.
  2. Listen for something recognizable and something changed. Is the response “like you,” or merely repeating you?
  3. Clear memory and try a contrasting register, rhythm or articulation. Compare what the system learns.

From musical conversation to learning environments

  • 2002–2003: the Continuator. François Pachet at Sony Computer Science Laboratory, Paris, developed a system that learned musical material in real time and generated stylistically related responses. The 2002 ICMC paper (PDF) and 2003 journal paper (author PDF) describe the original work. Performers played MIDI instruments; the musical exchange, rather than an on-screen piano, was central to the interface.
  • 2005–2006: young children as musical partners. Anna Rita Addessi and Pachet studied children’s exploration of the Continuator, including listening, repetition, variation and turn-taking. The studies below document these settings; they are not evaluations of this browser version.
  • Later educational development: MIROR. The MIROR platform extended reflexive interaction into improvisation (MIROR-Impro), composition (MIROR-Compo) and movement (MIROR-Body Gesture). Addessi’s 2020 overview explains the pedagogy and includes original interface illustrations. MIROR is a related educational platform, not another name for this page.
  • This study today. A new browser control surface runs the official Continuator 2.0 engine. It is not a reconstruction of the original screen or the full MIROR platform. The on-screen keyboard, piano roll and teaching prompts here are modern adaptations.

Watch original demonstrations with children and musicians on Pachet’s project page.

Published learning applications

Use these publications to examine the activities, participants and evidence behind reflexive musical interaction, rather than treating the software alone as a teaching method.

  • Preschool exploration: Addessi, A. R., & Pachet, F. (2005). Experiments with a musical machine: musical style replication in 3 to 5 year old children. British Journal of Music Education, 22(1), 21–46. An observational study of young children interacting with the Continuator. Author PDF.
  • Creative child–computer interaction: Addessi, A. R., & Pachet, F. (2006). Young children confronting the Continuator, an interactive reflective musical system. Musicae Scientiae, 10(1 suppl.), 13–39.
  • Beginning improvisation in primary school: Addessi, A. R., Anelli, F., Benghi, D., & Friberg, A. (2017). Child–Computer Interaction at the Beginner Stage of Music Learning: Effects of Reflexive Interaction on Children’s Musical Improvisation. Frontiers in Psychology, 8, 65. A MIROR-Impro study, not a test of this browser adaptation. Read with its published corrigendum.
  • Improvisation, composing and movement: Addessi, A. R. (2020). A Device for Children’s Instrumental Creativity and Learning: An Overview of the MIROR Platform. Frontiers in Psychology, 11, 516478. An open-access guide to the platform’s applications, theoretical framework and empirical studies.

These links lead to publications and historical documentation; they do not imply that the original MIROR software is hosted here or currently available to download.

Provenance, publications & teaching resources

This study uses François Pachet’s updated Continuator 2.0 engine, pinned at ec17dfd43f706938e97c28f3df592d1db4ebb4cc, and its pinned VO-Regular-BP dependency. It is a new browser interface, not a facsimile of the 2002 application. The original interaction principle is preserved through live learning and musical turn-taking. Synthesized sound replaces hardware MIDI synthesis; polyphony, precise timing and device latency require further comparative testing.
