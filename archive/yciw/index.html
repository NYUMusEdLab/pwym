The collaboration

Young Composers & Improvisors Workshop (YCIW), founded by music educator Matt McLean, brought a composition-first approach to school music: students learned musical concepts by developing their own pieces. McLean worked closely with MusEDLab, putting aQWERTYon and Groove Pizza into teaching practice and helping test Groove Pizza. The surviving record connects tool design with the everyday work of composing, improvising, notating and recording. [1] [2] [3] [8]

YCIW’s wider program paired composition with composer mentoring and performances by professional musicians. This profile focuses on the lab’s tool collaboration within that wider work. [8]

From playing
to composing

For students without piano technique, entering notes into notation software could separate composing from the physical experience of making music. McLean used aQWERTYon alongside Noteflight to close that gap. Students could try phrases on an ordinary computer keyboard, hear them against existing parts, and develop ideas before committing them to a score. A July 2016 MusEDLab article documented this work in both YCIW and McLean’s LREI classes. [2]

Groove Pizza offered a complementary starting point: a beat that students could hear, see and reshape. In the surviving classroom documentation, aQWERTYon improvisation happens over Groove Pizza rhythms, linking melodic exploration with beatmaking. [3] [4]

From exploration to a finished song

In a 2016 LREI project, McLean’s sixth graders composed and recorded original songs. Their workflow joined Noteflight’s notation with MIDI import into Soundtrap, where they could arrange, edit and record additional parts. McLean identified both aQWERTYon and Groove Pizza as useful ways into this process. Students also invited teachers, older students and family members to contribute performances. This was a particular LREI project, rather than a description of every YCIW classroom. [5]

The design lesson is a connected path: improvise an idea, shape its rhythm, develop it in notation or a recording environment, and hear it with other musicians. Accessible entry points can make ambitious musical work easier to begin.

A classroom partner
in tool design

McLean appears in Groove Pizza’s April 2016 play-testing credits. In May 2017, Ethan Hein described him as a MusEDLab partner and collected examples from his teaching: improvising over beats, playing with Noteflight, exploring chord melodies and inversions, and composing within a class period. Together, these records show a relationship that included tool use, testing and public documentation of classroom practice. [1] [3]

Selected milestones

These dates identify surviving documentation, rather than the formal start or end of the collaboration.

  • August 2015: YCIW publishes its aQWERTYon-to-Noteflight video tutorial. [9]
  • February 2016: MusEDLab’s account of aQWERTYon identifies McLean’s use of the instrument for open-ended composition. [6]
  • April–July 2016: Groove Pizza credits recognize his play testing; MusEDLab and McLean publish accounts of composition and songwriting with the tools. [1] [2] [5]
  • May 2017: Hein’s design study links five examples of McLean’s classroom work. [3]
  • December 2020: McLean’s chapter in The Music Technology Cookbook describes using aQWERTYon to create melodies and chord progressions with a DAW. [7]

People, tools
& context

  • Matt McLean / YCIW: composition curriculum and classroom practice; documented Groove Pizza play testing.
  • NYU Music Experience Design Lab: aQWERTYon and Groove Pizza; design and research documentation by Ethan Hein.
  • LREI: a documented classroom setting in McLean’s accounts, distinct from the wider YCIW program.
  • Noteflight and Soundtrap: connected notation and production tools in the documented workflow, developed outside MusEDLab.

Documentation
& provenance

This profile draws on contemporaneous MusEDLab writing, McLean’s teaching account, YCIW-published videos and a later academic chapter. McLean’s original YCIW discussion is preserved through the linked July 2016 MusEDLab article. The profile documents the collaboration; it does not restore the YCIW learning platform or establish a separate YCIW edition of the apps. Videos remain with their original publisher.
