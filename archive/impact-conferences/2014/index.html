Movement and music: Applications of motion sensors in music and multimedia art

Jarrod Ratcliffe; Adriana MaddenRead description

Venue: 6th Floor Conference

Printed abstract heading: Bodies in Motion: Applications of Motion Sensors in Music and Multimedia Art

Printed abstract credits: Jarrod Ratcliffe and Adriana Madden – NYU

Physical gesture has been used in the design of musical controllers since the invention of the theremin in 1919, but gestural input has never been as accessible or affordable for public consumption as it is right now. The recent introduction of affordable consumer sensors has opened up vast opportunities for modern musicians and multimedia artists to creatively explore and extend traditional paradigms of musical and artistic expression. Consideration is given to collaborative artistic uses of sensors, and their ability to augment music, dance, theater, and visual art. Such uses can be seen within performance, production, installation and educational contexts. This interactive session provides an overview of gestural controllers, and gives participants the opportunity to interact with motion-controlled multimedia systems designed by the presenters. There will also be an open discussion where participants will have the opportunity to brainstorm the possible integration of sensors into their own projects.

2014-08-14 · 10:00am EDT · Interactive session

Unpacking the DJ Mystique: An examination of the music making practices of EDM club DJs

Eva EgolfRead description

Venue: Room 879

Printed abstract credits: Eva Egolf – NYU/City School District of New Rochelle

Early 1980s dance music genres, such as house and techno, have transformed and fractured into a myriad of subgenres and practices. Collectively, these genres are often grouped by the moniker electronic dance music (EDM). EDM historically, and currently, is performed in dance clubs by its hero, the disc jockey (DJ). As the genre’s popularity has increased, it has emerged from underground clubland to become an ubiquitous art form. Within the last five years EDM’s status has grown in the United States with expanded radio air time, and prominent EDM DJs such as David Guetta, Armin Van Buuren, Avicii, Afrojack, and Skrillex etching a place in popular music’s consciousness with their productions appearing on Billboard’s The Hot 100 list of popular songs. The purpose of this interactive session is to explore the music making processes of EDM club DJs. This project draws on findings from a case study of five local EDM DJs. The interactive session explores mixing techniques using Elliott's (1995,2014) framework for musical understanding. In addition to mixing techniques, the music making context, and programming approaches are described. Fieldwork video footage is used to illustrate musical examples. This presentation utilizes the format of a lecture demonstration, with time allotted for audience questions and experimentation.

Music composition as experience: New composition strategies for the modern classroom

Matt McLeanRead description

Venue: Room 779

Printed abstract heading: Music Composition as Experience: New Composition Strategies for the Modern Classroom

Printed abstract credits: Matthew McLean - Young Composers & Improvisors Workshop at LREI

In the visual art class every student is an artist. Every student can now be a composer in music class. The art student develops skills and learns concepts through immediate experiences such as painting or drawing. With new technologies like the web app Noteflight and other iPad apps, music students can now benefit from the same kind of immediate experience. In this session, we’ll examine progressive techniques that help students explore music by being composers. The presentation includes video footage of students working and covers strategies for first time composers, through students working on their 4th compositions and beyond. The techniques can be summed up by the concept of "sound before theory." The strategies use technology or classroom instruments in a way that gives students the experience of “playing” with theoretical concepts in a hands on manner before having to codify or analyze them. We’ll examine how a project based composition project can meet every National Standard for music education. The presentation will also touch upon the three important components found to be necessary in successfully carrying out a composition curriculum on a classroom scale: a progressive curriculum, artists in residence and a community of online support. The strategies in this clinic have been realized while implementing a composition curriculum where since 2010 each and every 5th and 6th grade student at LREI has two compositions performed by professional musicians at two annual young composers concerts.

Creative play, musical learning, and middle school beta testers

Gena Greher; Eric RosenbaumRead description

Venue: 6th Floor Conference

Printed abstract heading: Creative Play, Musical Learning and Middle School Beta Testers

Printed abstract credits: Gena Greher – UMass Lowell & Eric Rosenbaum, MIT Media Lab

In collaboration with a graduate class action research project, we worked with middle school students using an iPad app called MelodyMorph that has a novel interface for making and playing musical instruments on the screen. The objective is to evaluate the teaching of musical concepts using this new technology in order to understand what they reveal about music cognition, and to develop ideas for future iterations of the technology. Music education students and the App designer brainstormed strategies for creating and introducing musical activities to the middle school students to support tune recognition, creating musical motifs and creating musical narratives. In addition to presenting several examples of student work, this hands-on interactive workshop will allow participants to explore several of the strategies that were developed over the course of this project, including the creation of musical puzzles to reconstruct tunes, creating music to accompany graphic drawings and musical soundscapes.

Maker, participatory, and digital cultures in the classroom

Ryan BledsoeRead description

Venue: 6th Floor Conference

Printed abstract heading: Maker, Participatory, and Digital Cultures in the Classroom

Printed abstract credits: Ryan Bledsoe – Arizona State University

In this session, we will experience what a combination of maker, participatory, and digital cultures can look like in the classroom. An emphasis will be placed on collaborative, project-based learning that spans across the disciplines of the STEAM subjects. People of all ages are makers: makers of food, sounds, instruments, computer programs, tangible items, and much more. An important component of making something is having the space to play, explore, and experiment with materials—something that research shows us young children learn well from, but not something we often incorporate into classrooms and education past early childhood. Play, exploration, and experimentation are also important components of maker and participatory cultures, which can be used in conjunction with digital tools and aspects of digital culture to engage the students in our classrooms in ways that are authentic to real-life artistic practices. In this experience, participants will creatively interact with music, sound, and visual art in an analog, digital, and/or acoustic manner. Participants will design and create a multimedia piece with tools appropriate for elementary-aged students. We will explore the possibilities of making and using non- traditional musical scores in collaborative, improvisational music making. By using a graphical or visual score, barriers are deconstructed so that people with varying degrees of musical instruction can participate. Additionally, by designing and building their own instruments during this session, participants will encounter decision making in music not often associated with K-12 music classes such as timbre selection, instrument design, and controller design.

2014-08-16 · 10:00am EDT · Interactive session

Record, upload, converse: Transforming assessment through Acclaim’s collaborative video platform

Jillian Harris Farrell; Aksel GungorRead description

Venue: Room 779

Printed abstract heading: Record, Upload, Converse: Transforming Assessment Through Acclaim's Collaborative Video Platform

Printed abstract credits: Jillian Harris Farrell & Aksel Gungor - Acclaim

It is easy to feel overwhelmed by all the different web tools available to instructors: social media, blogs, and polls—the list goes on. Finding the right tool or platform, however, can transform assessment in the classroom or studio in situations where rubrics and score sheets may be not only ineffective but also inefficient and unscalable. Acclaim is a web platform that facilitates discussion and feedback around video content. Professors and students can securely add videos to the platform (recorded content or curated videos from the web) and then post time coded comments that highlight and link to specific parts of each video. Put simply, the platform makes it easy to annotate video content. Access can be shared among classmates and/or professors for a private, collaborative, and ultimately, constructive learning experience. For this interactive session, we will address the challenge of assessing performance using traditional methods and show how Acclaim can enhance the precision of instructor feedback and generate a constructive dialogue between teachers and students. We will record the session’s attendees engaged in two separate live performance experiences. One group will learn and perform a simple dance phrase, while the other group will read an excerpt of a dramatic monologue. Using Acclaim’s features, each group will then provide commentary on one another’s performances. The session will conclude with a question and answer period and a discussion of Acclaim’s potential applications in realms like music education, trial law, public speaking, and medicine.

2014-08-16 · 10:00am EDT · Interactive session

Theory course for an all-online music education masters degree

Robinson McClellan; Stephen WilcoxRead description

Venue: Room 779

Printed abstract heading: Theory Course for All-Online Music Education Masters Degree

Printed abstract credits: Robinson McClellan & Stephen Wilcox - Rutgers University, Mason Gross Arts Online

BRING YOUR LAPTOP or IPAD This online masters course in music education at a large university serves to deepen the practical theory knowledge of future music educators while teaching them to teach via peer assessment and several online tools. IMPACT conference participants in our interactive session will complete a short musical analysis via the basic process used in our course design, in miniature form: • First, participants ‘Teach the Material’ by individually creating analyses of a short passage in (guided) blog form. • Students take the role of teachers for the second stage of the process, ‘Teach the Teacher,’ providing feedback on one another’s mini-blog analyses via a rubric. • Based on this feedback, each student creates a follow-up in the form of a description detailing how he or she would teach the piece to student performers, or a lesson plan. In the real course, instructors then grade students both for their analyses and for the quality of the teaching feedback they provide to their peers. Groups rotate through the semester in three-week cycles, creating multiple opportunities for interaction and sharing. All this work is bolstered by an online ‘textbook’ (written lessons) and semester-long assignments in which students read and react to scholarly articles on music analysis. Today’s teaching environment requires competence with basic music instructional technology such as notation software (for this course, Noteflight), web interactions and audio/video recording. These technologies and their uses in teaching are included as a built-in aspect of the coursework.

2014-08-16 · 1:00pm EDT · Interactive session

Dialogue with a controller: Projects, pilot-testing, and pedagogy

Jesse RathgeberRead description

Venue: 6th Floor Conference

Printed abstract heading: Dialogue with a Controller: Projects, Pilot-Testing, and Pedagogy

Printed abstract credits: Jesse Rathgeber – Arizona State University

Tools like Scratch and Makey-Makey afford us new ways to think about and create music. With these tools, we can construct new controllers and interfaces that allow us to play with our own sounds on our own terms. We can craft motion controllers that let us interact with sound through gesture. We can design touch interfaces that power interactive art installations. The possible outcomes of our interaction with these tools are diverse and imaginative. Yet, these creative products are but artifacts of intense, complex, and often meaningful creative processes. This session explores curricular applications of using Scratch and Makey-Makey to create interactive sound installations with a focus on the process of creating the works. We will explore a pedagogical framework for crafting such works that embraces pilot-testing, iterative design, and collaborative problem solving. Through hands-on engagement with these tools, we will uncover how such a framework can empower students to engage in dialogue with one-another, the potential users of their of their works, and even the works themselves.

2014-08-16 · 3:00pm EDT · Interactive session

Integrating Computer Music into your Secondary School Performance Ensemble

Evan CombsRead description

Venue: 6th Floor Conference

Printed abstract credits: Evan Combs – Connections Education/Juilliard

As a perpetual field, computer music has continued to develop in university music programs at an exponential rate. However, these technologies, theories, and implementations have been struggling to integrate into secondary schools. This session will explore the unique opportunities we, as music educators, can give students to explore and embrace computer music through ensemble performances, engaging Generation Z. Studying the accessible technologies of the twenty-first century classroom provides a better grasp of what tools and methods should be incorporated in our music curriculum, and available for performance use. Primarily focusing on the use of technology (laptops, smart devices, and video projection) as an instrument and controller in ensembles, the session will cover design methods and ways to develop your own software and app environments for creating and manipulating a variety of new data. This discussion will apply to Open Sound Control (OSC), cross-platform music technology software, and languages with open-source alternatives. Participants will take the provided steps to designing a secondary level computer music performance piece. When complete, the group will discuss and evaluate each project’s logistics, identifying its hardware and software requirements, accessibility, and educational value.

Sound and Sight

Jun LiRead description

Venue: Room 879

Printed abstract credits: Jun Li - Montclair State University

The digital evolution of communication and ever-increasing performance of the computer have transformed the visual communication design field into a discipline that synthesizes aural and visual, spatial and temporal elements. Digital technology provides new expressive possibilities for artists working in audio-visual media. The interactive session Sound and Sight engages visual representations of sound, space, and rhythm. Participants will select a sound sequence containing a variety of distinguishable motives and/or themes. The design process consists of identifying different dimensions of contrast in music and working in Max and Processing to simultaneously generate graphic compositions linking image and sound in a synaesthetic relationship. In addition, participants will edit the generated visual sequence, synchronize the audio, and add texts in Adobe AfterEffects.
