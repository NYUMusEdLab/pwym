003Participant 0032014-05-26#3

Thanks for starting this conversation, Glen. Our lesson from PWYM 1.0 is that we need to start thinking about redefining the C in MOOC to be community rather than course. The discourse around MOOCs, especially around issues of retention, essentially blame the student, rather than the course designers for not sticking around. The reality is that most people taking MOOCs have busy lives and may not be able to commit to a school-style week-to-week schedule. People end up falling behind, feel bad and opt to drop out. Some wait for the "next time," and for others that's the last they spend with the content.

What we've tried to do differently here in PWYM 2.0:

  • Bring the community here; while preserving links to the prior G+ community.
  • Establish a Facebook page and Twitter account for reaching members of our community closer to where they are on social media
  • Remove references to content organized by "weeks" and removed references to "due dates," instead encouraging a flexible path through the content
  • Ask the community to form their own groups by interest and affinity, as opposed to fixed initial grouping by musical taste (cool as that was!)
  • Created smaller, more bite-sized sets of content (you'll see in the new Modules 4 and 5 in particular) that you can skip over or go through more meticulously

We hope that the community will stay more active after the Peter Gabriel content finishes up. We plan on creating ways to sign up for the content whenever you want and to have the option to move through the content over a time period you'd like. We also want to open up to new pathways through the content as we add new artists to our set of partners.

Some of the "issues" with MOOC's I think are not really issues or there being blow out of proportion.

I don't see retention as an issue infact I think its one of the strengths of MOOC's. I have completed at least 5 classes on Coursera and I have dropped out of at least a dozen. I can "test" classes without worry, I would have never started let alone finished one class on Coursera if it had cost me money. Instead I completed 5. People sign to demo a class and stick around if they like the teacher/content,etc. Like me, the people that stick around would not have ever sign up if money was involved or if there was grade point penalty,etc

Also the study cited could very well be flawed based on how some of the MOOC's work. For example lets talk about the biggest MOOC Coursera, people "sign up" for classes there in a way that is not really signing up for a class in the traditional sense. But instead is more like bookmarking them.

I have looked at people Coursera profile pages that have them signed up for dozens of classes some not starting for many many months and often these people could have half a dozen or more classes overlapping each other. These people are not intending to take all these courses there just "bookmarked" so they get a notification when it starts and then they may decide to take or drop the class or perhaps "demo" the class to see if they like the teacher/format etc.

As for people with busy lifes well different MOOC's do things differently, go to Khan's Academy or Udacity its self paced you can finish in 3 days or 3 years its up to you. Personally I tend to prefer a bit more structure, the due dates that are part of Coursera's classes give me that extra little push to keep working consistently. But my point here is I don't think this is a major issue either, some prefer self pace, some prefer somewhat flexible pacing and some even prefer very rigid pacing. MOOC's provide a wide range of options and those option are growing as more free online courses pop up.

142Participant 1422014-06-11#7

Community is great and CAN be a huge advantage for a MOOC as long as all participants stay engaged. You can visit some product forums and feel like you are blowing dust off the furniture. Go can visit other forums and, in some cases, the community is more vibrant than the products supporting the forum. There doesn't seem to be that much rhyme or reason to why one achieves while others fail but I've noticed some traits in most of the successful forums. A strong cast of regular participants that enjoy helping new visitors learn and make new visitors feel like "one of the gang". Forum discussions do not have to be centered around one topic; for example a guitar forum will talk about all things music, not just guitars. The presence of moderators is felt more than seen. Moderators will step in to advance a conversation, solicit feedback, provide some (but not all) answers when it appears no one has an answer but otherwise let the regulars guide the discussion, even when responses are not correct. Sometimes an incorrect response will create a frenzy of discussion!

I'm glad to see there are some people, such as Glen, that are returning to the course for a second time. It will take people like him to make the PWYM community relevant over the long term.

I like MOOCs. It's a great way to explore things I find interesting and it keeps me thinking. Some courses I've gained more from the content, others more from the forums.

This is the first online course I've seen that is not worried about time. Proceed at your own pace really helps me but, to be honest, a little bit of time pressure can be a good motivator.

I like modules as a method to present material but would feel better if there were some self tests ever now and then so I can determine for myself if I understand the material. Self tests would also make for good subject matter within the community.

One question to Alex, how do you measure the success of a MOOC course? Here's my criteria; am I glad I invested my time, was I challenged and did I learn. If those three measures are met, it's been a good class for me.
