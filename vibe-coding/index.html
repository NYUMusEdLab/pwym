Start with your own groove

Take a groove into code

Our experimental Groove Pizza lets you carry a groove into live coding and vibe coding workflows. Open Download/Continue to copy or download editable code for Sonic Pi (.rb) or Strudel (.js). Paste it into the corresponding live coding editor and start changing the rhythm and sounds.

You can also take that exported code into an AI-assisted coding environment as a starting point for a new musical tool. Other environments may need adaptation.

Try experimental Groove Pizza ↗

Development preview. Code exports include selected pizzas, tempo and swing, and use Sonic Pi’s or Strudel’s sounds. The original kit sounds and reverb are not included.

Build your own music interface

Try a prompt

Ask an AI coding tool to build a new playable interface. You make the music; the coding tool writes the app. Each brief specifies controls, visual feedback, audio behavior, saving and working checks.

These briefs draw on MusEDLab tools, including our history of Scratch and hand-coded interfaces. Open a card, copy the complete prompt and adapt it. Inspect and test each prototype, then document your tools, design choices and possibilities for music learning. The prompts have not been tested through code generators; expect to iterate.

Submissions are not open yet

A showcase is taking shape.

We are preparing an on-site submission form. It is not accepting entries yet. In the meantime, build an experiment and document what you discover.

We welcome musical and teaching questions from educators, musicians, students and creative coders. A future submission should include:

  1. A working demo link. Give visitors something they can try, with any useful instructions.
  2. An image for display. Show the interface or musical idea and include a clear image credit.
  3. Your process and tools. Explain what you started from, how you built or remixed it, which tools—including any AI tools—you used, and what you tested.
  4. The teaching and musical possibilities. Describe what the design lets someone explore, make, hear or learn, and what still needs work.
  5. A title and creator credit. Include source acknowledgments and how you want the work credited.

Only share material you have permission to display. Leave out private recordings, student names and other personal data.

This initiative also supports MusEDLab’s developing NOHME chapter on vibe coding. Any future research participation or use of quotations would require a separate invitation and consent; display in a showcase does not provide research consent.

02 / Start with a question

What else could
this become?

Vibe coding brings conversation with AI into the process of making software. For us, the interesting question is what we can learn by making, playing and revising a musical tool.

A working result is a beginning. Look inside the code, ask how it works, test your assumptions and change one thing at a time. A tool may run and still make unexpected musical choices or leave someone out.

Listen

Make a possibility

What happens if a rhythm has another layer, a different cycle or a new sound?

Learn

Open another way in

Could a new interaction help someone explore, teach or participate?

Reflect

Explain the making

What did you change, how did you test it, and what surprised you?

Start from MusEDLab’s tools or a musical question of your own. Check the original code and asset licenses before reusing them, and credit your sources.

03 / Our Scratch roots

Look inside.
Make it your own.

Learning by remixing has a history here.

MusEDLab’s Scratch projects let learners play with musical ideas, inspect the blocks and reshape how a tool behaves. These earlier projects used block-based programming. Today’s AI-assisted experiments offer another route into the same cycle of making, listening and revising.

William Payne and S. Alex Ruthmann examined the possibilities and limitations of music making in Scratch in Music Making in Scratch: High Floors, Low Ceilings, and Narrow Walls? (May 16, 2019).

Independent AI-assisted experiment / Ethan Hein

Drum Pizza

Ethan Hein’s independent, vibe-coded Drum Pizza explores four drum layers, triplets and custom WAV or MP3 sounds. Its playful character recalls the early Groove Pizza prototypes before the formal 2016 Herbie Hancock Math Science Music launch.

Try a question: How does changing a subdivision or adding your own sound change the groove?
